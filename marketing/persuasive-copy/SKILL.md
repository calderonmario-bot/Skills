# Persuasive Copy

Most copy fails the same way. It **states value** when it should **create
wanting**. Those are different jobs and only one of them sells.

> Stating: "Free shipping on all orders. Authorized dealer. 30 day returns."
> Wanting: "Your back yard, 6am, steam coming off the deck."

Every fact in the first line is true, useful, and completely inert. The reader
learns something and feels nothing. The second line contains no information at
all and it is the one that sells.

## The one job: make them simulate it

People want what they can already picture themselves having. The mechanism is
mental simulation, and it is triggered by three things and blocked by their
opposites.

| Triggers simulation | Blocks it |
|---|---|
| A specific moment in time | A general state of affairs |
| Something you can see, hear or feel | An abstraction or a category |
| One person, one place | An audience, a market |

"Relaxing morning" is a category. "6am, steam coming off the deck" is a
Tuesday. Only one of them plays as a picture in someone's head.

This is the whole skill. Everything below is in service of it.

**Before writing anything, answer: what is the moment?** Not the benefit, not
the feature, the moment. A person, somewhere, at a time of day, and what has
just become possible for them. Write that down first even if it never appears
in the final copy, because copy written without it comes out as a list.

## Step 1: which job is this hired for

Nobody buys a product. They hire it to make something true about their life.
Pick the one that dominates. Copy aimed at three drivers at once hits none.

| Driver | The wanting underneath | Reads like |
|---|---|---|
| **Relief** | Make the bad thing stop | The problem, felt from inside, then the quiet after |
| **Identity** | Become the person who does this | A picture of that person, doing it, unremarkably |
| **Belonging** | Be with, or like, these people | A scene with other people in it |
| **Control** | Stop being at the mercy of it | The moment of knowing, versus the not-knowing before |
| **Pleasure** | Simply want it, no justification | Sensory, unapologetic, no reasoning offered |

Examples of the same product, different driver:

- Accounting software, **relief**: "It is the fourth of April and you are not thinking about it."
- Accounting software, **control**: "You will know what you owe in January, not April."
- Foster care nonprofit, **identity**: "You do not have to be extraordinary. You have to be there on Thursday."
- Foster care nonprofit, **belonging**: "There are eleven other people in the room who said yes too."
- HVAC company, **relief**: "The house is quiet again and nobody is talking about the upstairs bedroom."

Naming the driver first is what stops copy from becoming a shopping list. A
list is what you write when you have not chosen.

## Step 2: where is the reader

The same brand, the same voice, and a completely different job depending on
how much the reader has already decided.

| Stage | They are asking | Copy does | Getting it wrong looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold** (ad, bio, social, homepage hero) | Do I want this? | Creates wanting. Image, moment, one driver. | Specs, credentials, logistics. Answers a question they have not asked. |
| **Warm** (product page, service page, comparison) | Is this the right one, from these people? | Proves it. Specifics, mechanism, the awkward truths. | Vague warmth. They have decided they want it, now they want facts and you are still writing poetry. |
| **Hot** (pricing, checkout, booking, final CTA) | What could go wrong? | Removes risk. Terms, guarantee, what happens next, who to call. | Selling again. They are already sold. Answer the objection and get out of the way. |

**The most common single mistake in all of marketing** is running warm-stage
copy at a cold audience. Credentials, awards, "authorized dealer", "20 years
experience", feature lists. All of it is persuasive to somebody choosing
between vendors and invisible to somebody who has not decided they want the
thing at all.

## Step 3: the craft

### Image before information

Lead with something the reader can see. Information lands on a reader who is
already leaning in, and bounces off one who is not.

> Weak: "Our platform reduces month-end close time by up to 40%."
> Better: "Your controller went home at six on the last day of the quarter."

### Specificity is the engine of belief

Specific things sound true because only someone who was there would know them.
Vague things sound like marketing because only marketing talks that way.

> "Fast delivery" → "Thursday"
> "Affordable" → "$39"
> "Experienced team" → "We have done 1,400 of these"
> "Great customer service" → "You get my mobile number"

Round numbers read as estimates. Odd ones read as counted. Use the real number.

### Rhythm is the difference between human and machine

This is the one nobody teaches and it is the biggest single tell.

Machine copy has uniform sentence length. Every sentence lands with the same
weight, and the effect is flat and slightly menacing, like a form letter.

Human copy varies. A long sentence that unspools an idea and takes its time
getting where it is going, then a short one. Like that.

**Three clipped fragments in a row is the specific failure mode.** "No gym. No
booking. No drive." reads as a machine even though each fragment is fine. Two
is a rhythm. Three is a pattern, and a pattern is what a person notices instead
of the meaning.

Read every draft aloud. If you run out of breath, it is too long. If it sounds
like a countdown, it is too short.

### The turn

The best short copy has two beats: a setup and a pivot. The pivot is where the
personality lives.

> "Some of the best conversations happen at 180 degrees. **We put them in back yards.**"
> "Most people book a spa twice a year. **You could just walk out the back door.**"
> "We are not the cheapest. **We are the ones who answer the phone in January.**"

The turn does the selling, and because it arrives after the reader has already
agreed with the setup, it does not feel like selling.

### Write to one person

"Businesses like yours" and "our customers" describe a market. "You" describes
a person. Picture one real customer and write them a sentence.

### Earn the claim in the same breath

A claim without evidence is noise the reader has learned to skip. Attach the
proof to the claim rather than promising it later.

> Weak: "We are reliable."
> Earned: "We have not missed a Thursday in three years."

### Say the inconvenient thing

Admitting a real limit buys more credibility than any superlative, because it
signals you are not managing the reader. Pair it with what you will do.

> "We are not the cheapest option on this list, and if price is the deciding factor you should take one of the others. If what you want is somebody who picks up, that is the thing we are good at."

## Channel forms

Voice never changes. The shape does.

**Social bio (150 chars).** Two forms work and they are not the same.

*The moment*: an image and a turn. Best where the product is unfamiliar or the
appeal is hard to state. "Some of the best conversations happen at 180
degrees."

*The promise*: a specific claim in the first line, the category in the second.
Best on social, where a bio is read in under a second alongside a hundred
others and a stated payoff cuts through an image. "The 20 minutes that fix your
whole week. / Saunas and cold plunges, in your own back yard."

Test both. The promise form usually wins on social, the moment form usually
wins on a homepage where the reader has already arrived and has a second to
spare.

Either way, nothing operational. No shipping, no credentials, no "tap below"
when the platform already renders a link button an inch beneath the text. Spend
the second line on ownership or category, not logistics.

**Homepage hero.** One sentence of wanting, one sentence of orientation, one
button. The orientation line is the only place a cold reader needs to learn
what category you are in.

**Product or service page.** Warm stage. Open with the moment, then never be
vague again. This is where every spec, term and awkward truth belongs.

**Social post.** Open with the image or the turn, not the context. Nobody needs
the windup. The first line decides whether the rest gets read.

**Email.** One idea. A subject line that is a specific, not a tease. Write the
way you would to one person who already knows you a bit.

**Ad.** Cold stage, almost always. One driver, one moment. Resist listing.

## The rewrite loop

When copy is not working, diagnose before rewriting. The diagnosis is usually
one of six things.

| Symptom | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dry, informational | Stating value, not creating wanting | Find the moment. Write it. |
| Cold, curt | Unqualified "direct" with no warmth counterweight | Lengthen one sentence. Add a human aside. |
| Generic, could be any brand | No specifics | Replace every adjective with a number or a noun |
| Reads like AI | Uniform rhythm, or three parallel fragments | Vary sentence length. Break the pattern. |
| Technical, dry | Warm-stage copy at a cold audience | Move the specs to the product page |
| Salesy, hollow | Claims without evidence | Attach proof to each claim or cut it |

## Fail tests, before anything ships

1. **Is there a moment in it?** A person, a time, a place. If not, it is a list.
2. **Has this reader decided they want it?** If not, every specification is an interruption.
3. **Read it aloud.** Does it sound like a person talking, or a countdown?
4. **Count the negatives.** More than one "no" or "not" and you are describing absences instead of offering something.
5. **Could a competitor put their logo on it?** If yes, it says nothing.
6. **Is every claim earned in the same sentence?** If not, cut it or prove it.
7. **Is anything in it untrue, unverifiable, or a number you cannot source?** Hard fail. Fabricated proof is worse than no proof, legally and otherwise.

## Honesty is not a constraint on this, it is the method

Invented statistics, fake urgency, imaginary testimonials and borrowed
credibility all work briefly and then do not. More practically: the specificity
that makes copy persuasive has to come from somewhere real. You cannot write
"we have not missed a Thursday in three years" unless it is true, and the
search for true specifics is the same search as the search for good copy.

If a claim cannot be supported, the answer is to find a truer one, not a vaguer
one. Vague is where copy goes to die.
